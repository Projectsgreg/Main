What Fortune 500 Executives Get Wrong About Confidence

Gregory Swarn April 19, 2026 7 min read

Confidence is a feeling, and feelings are unreliable on exactly the days you need them most. Presence is a set of observable behaviors, and behaviors can be rehearsed until they hold under pressure — regardless of what is happening internally.

I have prepared executives for board presentations, investor pitches, and all-hands announcements where the stakes were high enough to make even seasoned operators nauseous in the elevator. Almost every one of them asked me some version of the same question beforehand: how do I get myself to feel confident before I walk in?

It's the wrong question. I have watched executives who felt terrified deliver a flawless fifteen minutes, and I have watched executives who felt perfectly composed walk into a room and lose it within ninety seconds. The feeling and the performance are not as connected as we assume. What predicts the outcome is not what is happening in the executive's chest. It's what their body is doing that the room can actually perceive.

The room doesn't know how you feel

This sounds obvious once stated, but almost no one operates as if it's true. A board, an investor, a room of direct reports — none of them have access to your internal state. They have access to your pace, your posture, the length of your pauses, the directness of your language. That's the entire data set they're working with. They build a read of your confidence entirely from behavior, because behavior is all that's visible.

Which means an executive waiting to feel confident before acting confident has the causality backwards. The feeling was never going to arrive on command — not before a board vote on a decision you're not fully sure about, not before telling investors a number came in short, not before informing your team about a restructure. Those moments produce genuine uncertainty. Waiting for the uncertainty to resolve into a warm, settled feeling before you perform is waiting for something that, most days, simply will not happen in time.

Presence has a body

What I coach instead is a shift from an internal target to an external one. Presence is not a state of mind you access. It's a small number of specific, physical, repeatable behaviors that a person can execute regardless of what they feel — and that, executed consistently, produce the read of confidence in the room whether or not the executive privately has it that day.

This is not a performance trick in the cynical sense. It works because presence and confidence are correlated in the room's perception precisely because most confident people, most of the time, exhibit these behaviors as a downstream effect of feeling settled. You are simply executing the downstream effect directly, upstream of the feeling, until the feeling often catches up on its own — which it frequently does, once the room responds well to what it's seeing.

The three markers

Across two decades of preparing executives for high-stakes rooms, three behavioral markers separate the ones who read as present from the ones who don't. None of them require feeling anything in particular.

Pace

Under pressure, almost everyone speeds up. The sentences compress, the breath shortens, the words start arriving slightly ahead of the thought. A room reads accelerating pace as anxiety within seconds, often before the speaker is consciously aware they've sped up. The correction is mechanical, not emotional: slow the rate of speech by roughly twenty percent from what feels natural in the moment. It will feel, to the executive, unnaturally slow. It will not sound that way to the room.

Pause

The instinct under pressure is to fill silence — to keep talking so the discomfort of the gap doesn't have room to register. But an unfilled pause before answering a hard question is one of the strongest presence signals there is. It communicates that the question was actually considered rather than deflected. I ask executives to build in a full two-count of silence before responding to anything difficult. It is one of the hardest behaviors to install, and one of the most reliable once it holds.

Directness

Under pressure, language softens. Qualifiers multiply. "I think maybe we should probably consider" replaces "we should." The room hears the hedging before it hears the content, and it reads the hedging as the real signal. Presence requires stripping the qualifiers even when the underlying certainty is genuinely partial. You can be honest about uncertainty in direct language — "I don't have full confidence in this number, and here's what I'd do regardless" reads as more present, not less, than a hedged version of the same sentence.

The room is not grading how you feel. It is grading what it can observe. Those are two different exams, and most executives are studying for the wrong one.

Rehearsing the behavior, not the feeling

The practical implication changes how you prepare for a high-stakes moment. Instead of trying to talk yourself into confidence beforehand — a strategy with an extremely poor track record under real pressure — rehearse the three markers directly. Say the hard sentence out loud at the slower pace. Practice sitting in the two-count of silence until it stops feeling unbearable. Draft the direct version of the difficult update and read it exactly as written, resisting the pull to soften it in the room.

None of this requires resolving how you feel about the situation. Most executives never fully resolve how they feel about the situation before they have to walk in. The ones who perform well anyway are not the ones who found a way to feel confident. They're the ones who stopped needing to.

Takeaway

Before your next high-stakes room, don't try to manufacture the feeling of confidence. Rehearse pace, pause, and directness instead. The feeling is optional. The behavior is not, and it's the only part the room can actually see.

Presence is trainable in a way that confidence, as a feeling, is not. That distinction alone has changed more boardroom outcomes for the executives I work with than any amount of pep talk ever did.
